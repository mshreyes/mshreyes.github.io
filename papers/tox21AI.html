Benchmarking AI models for predicting nuclear receptor activity from Tox21 assays

Chivukula N., Karthikeyan J., Thangavel H., Madgaonkar S.R., Samal A. (2026). Computational Toxicology 39, 100439.

The study compares machine learning, deep learning and transformer models, each with several chemical feature sets, on 43 nuclear receptor datasets curated from ToxCast invitrodb v4.3. The aim is to see how performance varies with dataset characteristics, not to crown a single best model. Class imbalance across datasets

Active chemicals are a minority in every dataset, which is why F1 was the primary metric. Bars above 10% active are highlighted.

Active was defined as hit-call ≥ 0.9; chemicals with ties between active and inactive replicates were dropped as inconclusive. A combined dataset labels a chemical active if it is active in either the agonist or antagonist assay, so it shows interaction with the receptor but not the mechanism.

Models and features

Architectures

Chemical features

Fingerprints are bit counts; descriptors are counts of values per chemical. DGCL also gets SMILES-derived molecular graphs; transformers take SMILES only.

  • Split: ML used 80/20 train/test with ten-fold cross-validation for tuning; DL and transformers used 80/10/10. Seeds 42, 123 and 1337; stratified.
  • Preprocessing: zero-variance filter, Boruta (top 100 features), SMOTE with 3 nearest neighbors for ML.
  • Applicability domain: DA index with a 95th-percentile threshold excluded 3–8% of test chemicals.

Counts as stated in Results 3.3.1: ML by feature type, then DL and transformers. Not mutually exclusive across families.

MoLFORMER beat ChemBERTa and MolRAG consistently; the authors attribute this to atom-wise tokenization and a larger pre-training set, which is a hypothesis rather than a tested result. No model architecture was consistently best across all datasets.

Chemical space and misclassification

Largest ClassyFire superclasses (of 8,430)

21 superclasses, 257 classes, 446 subclasses in total. All chemicals are organic.

Similarity network (Tanimoto > 0.5)

15,841 edges; 650 connected components with at least 2 chemicals.

About 43% of false negatives (missed actives) were isolated nodes in the network, versus about 39% of actives overall. The authors read this as limited structural analogs in training contributing to misclassification, and note it as a possible reason for MolRAG's low F1 (its retrieved reference molecules are less informative). Activity cliffs at a 0.5 similarity cutoff appeared in 38 of 43 datasets, but only 6 at 0.9. PR agonist had the highest share (32.54% of actives; 55 chemicals, 76 cliffs).

External validation

Representative models for AR, ERα and ERβ were tested on NTP ICE in vitro and in vivo data. Only 20–30 active chemicals were available per set.

Training chemicals were removed and chemicals with conflicting labels excluded. The ER in vivo data are rat uterotrophic assays, primarily ERα-driven, so the ERβ result does not reflect ERβ-specific activity.

Comparison with published models

207Scopus records (searched 16 March 2026)
49used nuclear receptor Tox21 data
13reported F1 scores

Of those 13: 5 used ToxCast invitrodb (3 on v4.1, 1 on v3.2, 1 on v2), 6 used the Tox21 Data Challenge set, and 2 used CERAPP or PubChem bioassays. Against studies using invitrodb, this study reported higher F1 for the majority of assays, mostly from RF or XGBoost with descriptors. For several endpoints (for example GR agonist, 0.85 in an invitrodb v2 study) earlier F1 values were higher. The authors stress that dataset versions, preprocessing and imbalance handling differ, and that training/test partitions of earlier studies were unavailable, so the comparison is not controlled.

Limitations stated by the authors

  • Descriptor failures drop chemicals; models cover organic compounds only.
  • The features behind descriptor-based gains were not identified, and other descriptor sets were not tested.
  • SMOTE samples may not reflect the true minority distribution in severely imbalanced datasets.
  • SVM failed to converge on some feature sets, leaving missing predictions for those splits.
  • Three random splits may not capture variability; stratified splitting is questionable for extremely imbalanced sets. The "representative" model is the top test F1, not a claim of best model.
  • DA-index distance metrics differ by feature space, which limits comparison of AD-corrected performance.
  • External validation covers only AR and ER with few actives, and ER data lack subtype annotation.
