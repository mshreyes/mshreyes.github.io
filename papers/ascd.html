Network toxicology focused investigation on the impacts of inorganic arsenic and cadmium on human and ecosystem health

Chivukula N., Madgaonkar S.R., Ramesh K., Mangot S., Karthikeyan P., Marigoudar S.R., Sharma K.V., Samal A. (2026). Environmental Toxicology and Chemistry 45(2), 468–489.

The study links 62 inorganic arsenic and 18 inorganic cadmium compounds to Indian exposure pathways (AEPs), to adverse outcome pathways (AOPs), to species via ECOTOX, and to hazard thresholds and risk quotients for Indian rivers, all from a One Health perspective.

Aggregate exposure pathways

An AEP is a directed network from source to target site: nodes are key exposure states (KESs), links are key transitional relationships (KTRs). These were built from India-specific literature.

Stressor-AOP networks

AOPs came from AOP-Wiki (April 2024): 342 high-confidence AOPs with 1,163 KEs and 1,827 KERs. Only 35 are endorsed and 13 under review, so the authors treat complete AOPs as plausible hypotheses rather than limit themselves to endorsed ones.

Directed network hubs (degree)

Beyond the four AOPs already linked to each metal in AOP-Wiki, the data-centric approach identified 47 additional arsenic-AOPs and 74 additional cadmium-AOPs. Taxonomic applicability was annotated in AOP-Wiki for 28 of 48 arsenic-AOPs and 42 of 72 cadmium-AOPs in the largest components. For AOPs lacking it, applicability was inferred where every KE belongs to one taxonomic group: 8 arsenic-AOPs and 8 cadmium-AOPs.

Stressor-species networks (ECOTOX)

Networks link compounds to tested species by acute toxicity (LC50/EC50, 24–96 h), chronic toxicity (NOEC) or bioconcentration factor.

Species linked

Hazard thresholds (HC05)

HC05 is the concentration hazardous to 5% of species; lower means more toxic. The paper reports the values in supplementary tables, so this page summarizes the findings rather than the numbers. SSDs need data from at least five ECOTOX species groups.

Species were called sensitive to a compound when their toxicity value fell below that compound's HC05, and this was overlaid on the stressor-species networks. Across both endpoints, cadmium compounds were more toxic than arsenic compounds, and arsenites (arsenic oxide, sodium arsenite) were more toxic than arsenates. The toxicity-normalized SSD (SSDn) pools chemicals by normalizing to a reference species and gave consistent HC05 estimates, except for chronic cadmium.

Risk quotients in Indian rivers

RQ is the measured concentration (Central Water Commission 2019 report, sampled before 2019) divided by a PNEC. The PNEC is the geometric mean of compound-specific PNECs. RQ > 1 potentially indicates ecological risk.

Paired comparisons (Wilcoxon signed-rank effect size)

All three differences were significant (p = 7.5e-18, 2.3e-4 and 1.0e-21). Monsoon RQs were lower than non-monsoon RQs, attributed by the authors to dilution from higher river flow. Arsenic RQs were lower than cadmium RQs across 135 paired stations, so cadmium potentially poses the higher risk.

The PNECs were comparable to, or more stringent than, existing water quality criteria elsewhere. India has drinking-water guidelines of 10 µg/L (arsenic) and 3 µg/L (cadmium) but no environmental water quality criteria for aquatic life.

Limitations stated by the authors

  • No data on pH, temperature or salinity limit how far results apply; speciation was rarely reported.
  • AEP networks depend on the geographic spread of sampling sites and on varied measurement techniques that are hard to harmonize.
  • India-specific toxicity data in ECOTOX are limited, so some ecological groups are under-represented, and SSD results are not geographically specific.
  • The larger fold change between SSDn and single-compound HC05 for cadmium may mean the most sensitive available species were not sensitive enough.
  • The approaches cannot assess bioaccumulation across trophic levels.
  • The river data predate 2019 and may not reflect current levels.
